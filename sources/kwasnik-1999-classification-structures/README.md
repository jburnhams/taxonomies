## What is distinctive

The clearest comparison of the shapes a classification can take, and what each demands of your knowledge.

## Notes

Checked against the stored text 2026-11-25. Page numbers are *Library Trends* 48(1).

Four structures, each with what it needs and where it breaks:
- **Hierarchy** (pp. 25-29): strict is-a, inheritance down the levels, mutually exclusive classes, rules of division fixed a priori. Gives economy (attributes inherited, not repeated), inference from partial evidence, and a high-level view. Breaks on: **multiple hierarchies** (a dog is a mammal *and* a pet), **multiple and diverse criteria** (lions in the wild vs in zoos; dinosaurs by age or by kind), and **lack of complete knowledge**: "they require relatively complete knowledge of the domain in advance". Her test for a hierarchy built too early: "The sure sign of a 'premature' hierarchical structure is the need for a 'miscellaneous' or 'other' category" (pp. 28-29).
- **Tree** (pp. 29-33): divides by rules like a hierarchy "but does not assume the rules of inheritance"; the relation can be part-whole, rank, process stage. Same need for fairly complete knowledge.
- **Paradigm** (pp. 33-39): entities "described by the intersection of two attributes at a time" (her example: kinship terms by gender, English vs Polish). **Empty cells** prompt the question whether a concept is missing or the axes are wrong. Limits: needs domain knowledge to choose axes, gives one lens ("a view through a particular lens"), and is only one level deep, so descriptive rather than explanatory. Strictly two dimensions, not "two or three" as the first pass said.
- **Faceted analysis** (pp. 39-42): "not really a different representational structure but rather a different approach to the classification process". Steps: choose facets, develop values within each, analyse entities into facet strings, optionally a citation order (only needed for physical arrangement). Strengths: does not need complete knowledge or a strong theory, hospitable to new entities, flexible (post-coordination), each facet can have its own vocabulary, allows multiple perspectives. **Weaknesses**: choosing the facets is hard without knowing the domain and the users, and "it is not a simple matter to add fundamental facets once the general classification is established"; facets are not related to each other ("each facet functions as a separate kingdom"); hard to visualise more than one or two at a time.

Her point that matters for us: each structure claims a different amount of **theory** about the domain. A hierarchy claims you know it fully; facets claim less but put all the risk on the choice of facets, which is hard to change later. Pick the structure your knowledge can support, and spend the effort on the facets.
