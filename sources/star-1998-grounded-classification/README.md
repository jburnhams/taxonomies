## Notes

Checked against the stored text 2026-11-25; page numbers are *Library Trends* 47(2).

- **The shared problem** (pp. 218, 222): grounded theory (Glaser and Strauss) and Ranganathan's faceted classification both try to represent "vernacular words and processes, empirically discovered", in a way that is "ethnographically faithful (faithful to the needs of users and particular populations), yet simultaneously powerful beyond the single instance or case study".
- **Both reforms** (p. 222): each began as a reform against "powerfully entrenched a priori schemes with claims on universality", without giving up formalising and systematising.
- **Constant comparison** (grounded theory): seek similarities across unlike cases to reveal the dimensions in a situation (Hughes's "why is a priest like a prostitute?"). Categories move from substantive to formal as comparison goes on, and stay open to revision.
- **What each lends the other** (pp. 223-224): grounded theory brings processes and actions into vernacular representation; faceted classification offers "a way to assess the structural integrity and architecture of a particular theory, via facet analysis".
- For us: this is the empirical-to-conceptual half of our build method. Gather the words people actually use (vernacular, from the repos and from Jonathan), compare across cases to find the facets, then check the result with facet analysis. Constant comparison is a concrete technique for the step "find the facets" that Kwasnik says is the hard part.
