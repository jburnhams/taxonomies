## Notes

Checked against the stored text 2026-11-25. Page numbers are *Cognitive Psychology* 8.

- **Definition** (abstract): basic objects are "the most inclusive categories whose members: (a) possess significant numbers of attributes in common, (b) have motor programs which are similar to one another, (c) have similar shapes, and (d) can be identified from averaged shapes of members of the class".
- **Part II** shows basic objects are the most inclusive categories for which a concrete image of the whole category can be formed, the first categorisations made in perception, "the earliest categories sorted and earliest named by children", and "the categories most codable, most coded, and most necessary in language".
- **The tree surprise** (pp. 392-393): for the biological taxonomies (tree, fish, bird), "the basic level appeared to be the next higher level in the taxonomy than had been initially proposed by anthropological and linguistic-taxonomic evidence" (Berlin's genus level, oak and maple). So for these American students *tree* behaved as basic, not *oak*.
- **Expertise** is raised, not tested: "the degree of expertise of subjects did not vary systematically" (p. 393). The paper's evidence is one anecdote, a former airplane mechanic whose attributes, motor programs and canonical view of airplanes were far finer than other subjects' (late discussion), and it says "The effects of expertise on classification requires systematic study." The first pass stated "expertise moves the basic level down" as a finding; it is Rosch's hypothesis here, tested by later work (for example Tanaka and Taylor 1991, not stored; from prior knowledge, check).
- For us: the basic level depends on who the names are for. Name a vocabulary's everyday terms at the level its actual users would, and expect experts to want a finer level.
