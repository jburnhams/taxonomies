## What is distinctive

People behave as if categories have hidden essences, even when they cannot say what they are.

## Notes

Checked 2026-11-25 by reading the stored scan (pp. 179-195; no text layer, so quotes are transcribed by eye).

- **Claim** (p. 180): "psychological essentialism" is "the idea that these surface features are frequently constrained by, and sometimes generated by, the deeper, more central parts of concepts". Similarity on surface features alone cannot explain why people class whales with bears.
- **Not metaphysics** (p. 184): "The point about psychological essentialism is not that it postulates metaphysical essentialism but rather that it postulates that human cognition may be affected by the fact that people believe in it."
- **Essence placeholder** (pp. 184-185): people's concepts "may contain what might be called an *essence placeholder*". They need not know what the essence is; the placeholder may hold a partial theory, or just the belief "that there are people, experts, who really know what makes the thing the thing that it is". "Just as with theories, what the placeholder contains may change, but the placeholder remains."
- **Evidence they cite** (p. 184): Rips's experiments, in which people would not reclassify an object after superficial transformations made it look like another category. The painted-raccoon example in the first pass is Keil's (1989), not this chapter's; it illustrates the same thing.
- **Graded, not two-tier** (p. 185): properties lie "on a continuum of centrality ranging from relatively inaccessible, deep properties to more accessible, surface ones".
- For us: people treat a category name as pointing at a hidden "real" nature, which is why renaming feels like changing what things are, why naming disputes run hot, and why there is a pull toward one "true" taxonomy. The placeholder that defers to experts also suggests a practical move: a glossary entry with an owner who "really knows" gives that deference somewhere to land.
