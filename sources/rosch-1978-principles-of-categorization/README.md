## What is distinctive

Why some levels of a taxonomy feel natural and others do not, and what prototypes are (and are not).

## Notes

- **Two principles** (p.2): **cognitive economy**, "the task of category systems is to provide maximum information with the least cognitive effort"; and **perceived world structure**, the world comes "as structured information rather than as arbitrary or unpredictable attributes".
- **Vertical** dimension (level of inclusiveness: collie, dog, mammal, animal) and **horizontal** dimension (dog vs cat vs car at one level) (p.4).
- **Basic level** (p.4): "the most inclusive (abstract) level at which the categories can mirror the structure of attributes perceived in the world". Example (Table 2.1): furniture > **chair** > kitchen chair. Evidence converges on it from attribute lists, shared motor movements, shape overlap, averaged shapes, imagery, speed of recognition, the first words children learn, and the word adults use to name a picture (pp.6-10).
- **Prototypes** (p.11): "the clearest cases of" membership, measured by people's goodness ratings. But she warns the idea "has tended to become reified as though it meant a specific category member or mental structure", and that "prototypes themselves do not constitute any particular model of processes, representations, or learning" (p.15).
- For us: name things at the basic level for the people who will use the names, and define categories with clear examples as well as rules.
